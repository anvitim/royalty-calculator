# Who gets paid when a song is streamed? (India)

**Draft for agreement. Sources checked 6 October 2026.** Services and societies in India have changed a lot since 2024, so every status below is as of that date.

**Who this is for:** an independent artist in India who writes and releases her own non-film songs, has no label and no publisher, and releases through a distributor (a company that gets the music onto Spotify, JioSaavn, Apple Music and other services). Money is in rupees (₹), written the Indian way (₹1,00,000; lakh and crore). A stream count is written both ways the first time it appears in each section, for example "10 lakh (1 million) streams".

**FACT** means the linked source states the figure, and I opened that source. **ASSUMPTION** means a typical figure, an estimate, or a source figure applied to a situation it doesn't directly cover; each one says why and how unsure it is. Where the only source I could open is a news report, I've tagged it ASSUMPTION and said so, even if it's probably right.

A song earns money in two ways: from **the recording** (the audio file) and from **the songwriting** (the words and tune). Here the artist is both, so they are owed both. The two are collected by different routes, and only the songwriting route needs the artist to join something.

## 1. The route the money takes

**In two sentences.** Listeners (or advertisers) pay a streaming service; the service keeps about a third, then pays the recording money to the distributor, which passes it to the artist, and pays the songwriting money to IPRS, which pays only its members. The artist joins IPRS themselves to get the songwriting money, and may also join a performers' society and a recording society for radio, TV and venue play, which don't come through streams.

| Step | Who | What happens | Label |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Listener or advertiser → streaming service | Paying listeners give a monthly fee (Spotify Premium Standard is ₹139 a month). Free listeners are paid for by adverts. | **FACT:** [Spotify India](https://www.spotify.com/in-en/premium/) |
| 2 | Streaming service keeps its cut | About one-third. | **ASSUMPTION** (see section 3) |
| 3 | Recording money → distributor → artist | The distributor passes it on. | **FACT** for DistroKid ("100% of earnings", [DistroKid](https://distrokid.com/pricing/)) and for TuneCore India ("you keep 100% of your royalties", with a 20% fee on social-video money, [TuneCore India](https://www.tunecore.com/en-in/pricing)) |
| 4 | Songwriting money → IPRS → artist | Streaming services pay IPRS under licences. IPRS pays its members, four times a year. | **FACT:** [IPRS distribution rules](https://iprs.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/Distribution-Rules-Methods.pdf) |
| 5 | Radio, TV, shops, venues | Collected by societies for recordings and performers. Not streaming money. | See section 1.2 |

**Joining IPRS is not enough: each song has to be registered too.** IPRS pays by matching what each streaming service reports against the songs in its own database. Its rules say works that can't be matched, or aren't fully documented, are held back: the money sits in an "unidentified uses" account, and a member has three years from the date of the original distribution to claim it by submitting the works. **FACT:** [IPRS distribution rules](https://iprs.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/Distribution-Rules-Methods.pdf). IPRS's own annual report says the same from the other side. Its "four pillars" are membership, documentation (metadata), licensing and distribution. The metadata it needs is contributor names, role splits and the song codes (ISRC, ISWC and IPI), and "when metadata is missing or incorrect: royalties are lost or delayed". **FACT:** [IPRS annual report 2024-25](https://iprs.org/wp-content/uploads/Annual%20Report-FY2024-2025.pdf). The report names independent creators as the ones who most often have gaps. I read "register every song after joining" from these two passages; neither says in so many words that a member must register each song first, and I couldn't open IPRS's member pages to see its portal steps. The worked examples assume every song is registered.

**Things I couldn't pin down:**
- **Which performers' society is registered today.** ISRA was registered in 2013 and renewed in 2018 for five years. ISAMRA applied for registration in September 2023. A law firm's list of registered societies dated 31 August 2026 shows ISAMRA's application as still pending. I couldn't confirm either way from the Copyright Office.
- **Whether streaming money reaches performers at all.**

**What IPRS costs to run, once you're in.** In 2024-25 IPRS spent ₹38.7 crore of its ₹741.6 crore income on running costs, so **5.2%**. **FACT:** [IPRS annual transparency report](https://iprs.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/Annual-Transparency-Report-FY2024-25.pdf) (the 5.2% is my division of its two figures). It pays out four times a year (June, September, December, March), and doesn't pay amounts under ₹100 after tax. **FACT:** [IPRS distribution rules](https://iprs.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/Distribution-Rules-Methods.pdf)

**How IPRS splits streaming money.** For online services, IPRS allocates performing and copying rights equally, and pays by plays logged by each service. **FACT:** same rules. It had over 17,000 members at March 2025. **FACT:** [IPRS annual report 2024-25](https://iprs.org/wp-content/uploads/Annual%20Report-FY2024-2025.pdf)

## 2. The value of one stream in India

This is the hardest number. India has no published average rate per stream, and the Spotify-style figures online are blog guesses.

**What is known:**
- Spotify doesn't pay a fixed rate. It divides a pool by each track's share of streams in that country, and **payouts differ by the listener's country and plan.** **FACT:** [Spotify royalties guide](https://artists.spotify.com/royalties-guide)
- Streams from paying users add more to the pool than streams from free users, whose plays are paid for by adverts. **FACT:** same page.
- About two-thirds of Spotify's music revenue goes to recording and publishing, about four-fifths of that to recording and one-fifth to publishing. **FACT:** same page. This is Spotify's own worldwide statement, not an Indian figure.
- In 2025, India's paid music subscriptions earned **₹1,030 crore**, from **1.44 crore (14.4 million)** subscriptions, and there were about **16,400 crore (164 billion) paid streams**. **ASSUMPTION** (low uncertainty): FICCI–EY report as reported by [Music Business Worldwide](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/india-added-nearly-4m-paid-music-streaming-subscriptions-in-2025-taking-its-total-to-14-4m-according-to-new-report/).
- The same report counts about **6 lakh crore (6 trillion)** streams in all, so paid streams are **under 3%** of the total (164 billion ÷ 6 trillion = 2.7%). **ASSUMPTION** (low uncertainty): same source.
- Free (advert-paid) income per stream isn't published. **Gap.**

**Why the share of paying listeners for one artist's audio streams is probably higher than 3%.** The 6 lakh crore (6 trillion) total is almost six times the on-demand streams a Music Business Worldwide report gives for 2023 (about 1.04 lakh crore, or 1.037 trillion; [source](https://musicbusinessworldwide.com/hungama-music-shutters-streamings-service-marking-third-major-exit-from-india)), so it must count much more than audio services, probably video such as YouTube. If the audio count grew about 15% a year, which is the FICCI–EY growth for all streams, it would be about 1.4 lakh crore (1.4 trillion) in 2025, and 164 billion paid streams would be about 12% of it. **ASSUMPTION, uncertain:** the growth rate and the idea that the 6 trillion includes video are mine.

**Working (my own sums, so ASSUMPTION):**

| Working | Calculation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| A. What one paid stream brings in | ₹1,030 crore ÷ 16,400 crore | **₹0.063** (6.3 paise) |
| Check on A | 16,400 crore ÷ 1.44 crore subscriptions ÷ 365 | about 31 streams a day each: believable |
| B. What one free stream brings in | A ÷ 5 | **₹0.013**. **ASSUMPTION, very uncertain:** I found no source. One-fifth is a guess; it could be a tenth or a third. |
| C1. Paying share of all streams, India-wide | 164 billion ÷ 6 trillion | **2.7%** (under 3%) |
| C2. Paying share of audio streams only | 164 billion ÷ about 1.4 trillion | **about 12%** (see the paragraph above) |
| C3. Mix I chose for the typical case | 10% paying, 90% free | **ASSUMPTION, uncertain:** between C1 and C2, a little below C2 |
| **Typical value of one stream** | 10% × ₹0.063 + 90% × ₹0.013 | **about ₹0.018 (1.8 paise)** |
| Low | 5% paying, free worth a tenth of paid: 5% × ₹0.063 + 95% × ₹0.0063 | about ₹0.009 |
| High | 30% paying, free worth a third of paid: 30% × ₹0.063 + 70% × ₹0.021 | about ₹0.034 (₹0.0336 from these rounded figures; ₹0.0335 from unrounded ones) |
| Ceiling | every stream from a paying listener | ₹0.063 |

**Does the low case still make sense against C1?** Yes, with a caveat. The low case uses 5% paying, which is above India's 2.7% (C1) but below the audio-only 12% (C2). If an artist's listeners were as free-heavy as India overall (2.7% paying, free worth a tenth of paid), the value would be about ₹0.008, just under my low case. That is why the calculator's slider goes down to ₹0.005.

**Result: ₹0.018 a stream, with a range of ₹0.009 to ₹0.034. ASSUMPTION, and the least certain number in this file.** Reasons:
- the 164 billion paid-stream count may cover more than audio;
- the two reports count different things (above);
- ₹1,030 crore may include GST (tax charged to subscribers).

**What the calculator user will be able to change:** the value of one stream, from ₹0.005 to ₹0.065 (the top is the all-paying ceiling), starting at ₹0.018 (range ₹0.009–₹0.034), and the cost of making and promoting the release, starting at ₹0 with no suggested figure.

A blog quotes about ₹0.07 a stream for Spotify India. **ASSUMPTION, not used:** the blog gives no method, and ₹0.07 is about the all-paying ceiling, not an average.

**Other rules:**
- On Spotify, a track earns recording money only after 1,000 streams in 12 months. **FACT:** [Spotify](https://artists.spotify.com/royalties-guide) (stated worldwide; I haven't confirmed it applies the same way in India).
- IPRS doesn't collect a share for non-members; it holds it. See section 1.2.

**Result:** an artist-writer who joins IPRS, registers their songs and uses a distributor receives **about 66 paise of every ₹1** the service brings in:
- 53 paise from the recording;
- about 13 paise from the songwriting, after IPRS's running costs.

Without IPRS, only the 53 paise reaches them from streams.

## 4. What Indian law says about the songwriter

**What the law guarantees. FACT:** the [Copyright Act, 1957](https://www.indiacode.nic.in/bitstream/123456789/1367/1/a195714.pdf) (I couldn't open the government copy; I checked the wording below against the full text in [this published copy](https://www.bananaip.com/acts-rules/copyright-act.pdf)).
- **Section 17(a):** the author of a work is its first owner. So a songwriter who isn't an employee owns their words and tune from the moment they write them.
- **Section 18(1), fourth proviso (added 2012):** the author of a musical or literary work included in a sound recording that isn't part of a film "shall not assign or waive the right to receive royalties to be shared on an equal basis with the assignee of copyright for any utilization of such work except to the legal heirs of the authors or to a collecting society for collection and distribution and any assignment to the contrary shall be void". (I called this the second proviso in the first draft. The Act's provisos to 18(1) run: future works, new media, film works, then this one, so it is the fourth.)
- **Section 19(10), the same rule from the other side:** "No assignment of the copyright in any work to make a sound recording which does not form part of any cinematograph film shall affect the right of the author of the work to claim an equal share of royalties and consideration payable for any utilization of such work in any form."
- The protection for film songs is a separate pair: the third proviso to section 18(1) and section 19(9).

**Does it change anything for someone who releases their own songs? Almost nothing, today.**
- The guarantee is against a label or publisher taking the royalty by contract. A self-releasing artist hasn't assigned anything, so there is no "assignee" to share with, and they keep all of it.
- It **doesn't** pay the artist anything by itself. The money still arrives only through IPRS, and only if they have joined and registered the song.
- It starts to matter if they later sign to a publisher or label: they could not sign away the right to be paid their half of the royalty.

**Two other sections:**
- **Section 31D** (**FACT:** same Act) lets a "broadcasting organisation" communicate published songs and recordings "by way of a broadcast or by way of performance" at a royalty rate fixed by the court. The words don't mention the internet. Whether streaming services can use it was decided in court: in *Tips Industries v Wynk Music* the Bombay High Court held on 9 May 2019 that section 31D covers only radio and television, not internet streaming, so streaming services need ordinary licences. **ASSUMPTION** (low uncertainty): that is an interim ruling of one High Court, which I know only from a [SpicyIP report](https://spicyip.com/2019/05/breaking-bombay-high-court-rules-against-statutory-licensing-for-online-streaming-services.html). I couldn't open the judgment, and I haven't checked for later appeals or other courts. It fits with IPRS's income: streaming services paid it ₹602.4 crore under licences in 2024-25 (section 1.2).
- **Section 38A** gives performers the exclusive right over reproducing and communicating their performance to the public. **FACT:** same Act. How performers get paid in practice for a self-released recording is the open point in section 1.2.

## 7. What's left out

- **YouTube and short-video apps (Instagram Reels, and similar).** Left out. **This distorts India more than the UK.** In India 32% of survey respondents used YouTube for music, and discovery increasingly happens on short-form video and social platforms (both from the [EY press release](https://www.ey.com/en_in/newsroom/2026/07/india-s-paid-music-subscriptions-could-reach-28-30-million-by-2028-as-industry-focuses-on-monetization-and-premium-experiences)). An independent artist may earn or lose more there than on audio services, and those payments follow different rules. **FACT:** TuneCore India charges a **20% fee** on money from TikTok, Facebook, Instagram and YouTube, unlike the 0% on the music services ([TuneCore India](https://www.tunecore.com/en-in/pricing)). So even the distributor's own terms differ for this money, and the calculator's "artist keeps all of it" wouldn't hold there.
- **The cost of making and promoting the release** (recording, artwork, marketing). Left out by default: the worked examples and the break-even figures cover only the distributor's fee, so they understate what a release really costs. I found no source for a typical figure, so the calculator starts at ₹0 and suggests none; the reader can enter her own, and it is then taken off "What you keep" and counted in the break-even.
- **Free listening.** Included only through the single value of one stream. It matters far more in India than in the UK, since only about 8% of streamers pay.
- **Film music.** Left out; film songs have a separate royalty rule (the third proviso to section 18(1) and section 19(9)) and the industry is film-led.
- **Radio, TV, shops, pubs and live shows**, and the societies that collect them. Left out, apart from naming them in section 1.2.
- **Ringback tones, downloads and sync (use in adverts, films and games).** Left out. IPRS collects for them.
- **Tax.** GST on the distributor fee is included (section 5). Left out: tax taken off IPRS payments (IPRS's rules say it deducts tax), income tax, and whether the artist must register for GST themselves. Foreign artists would be taxed differently.
- **Streams outside India.** Left out. Songs released in India can also be streamed overseas, where a stream may be worth more, and the money arrives in other currencies.
- **A publisher or co-writers.** Left out. This artist writes alone and has no publisher.
- **Currency.** Everything is in rupees, except DistroKid's price in dollars.
